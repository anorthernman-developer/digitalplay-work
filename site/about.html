About the project

Play first. Everything else follows.

digitalplay.work is a free, open collection of creative tools for people who would rather make something than scroll past it.

What this is

It’s a shelf of small, playful tools that run in your web browser. Each one takes a single creative idea, like cutting up a sentence, folding a drawing into a kaleidoscope or growing a pattern from a few rules, and turns it into something you can push about with your own material.

The tools are grouped into twelve sections, from Music and Art to Making and Nature & Science. 5 of them have tools in already. The rest are open for ideas, and you can suggest what goes in them.

Why “digital play”

The name is borrowed, with love, from a book of the same name about using computer games to teach languages. That book argues that play isn’t a break from learning, it’s one of the best ways to do it, and that what matters is what you do with the technology rather than how fancy it is. We think the same is true for making art, music and writing.

Fun first, then the clever bit

Every tool has to be fun within a minute of opening it. Only then does it show you the idea underneath: where the technique came from, why it works, and how to take it further. Innovation tends to turn up when people are messing about, so we make the messing about as easy as we can.

Free, and staying free

Everything here is given away. No accounts, no adverts, no paywall, no upsell. The code is under the MIT licence and the words and pictures are under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0, so you can copy a tool, change it, use it in a classroom or a workshop, and share it again. All we ask is a credit.

Get off the socials for a bit

Scrolling is easy, and it’s designed to keep you scrolling. Making something, even something small and daft, uses a different part of your brain. You make choices, you get things wrong, you notice things. That’s the part we want to wake up. So there’s no feed here, no likes and no notifications. Open a tool, make something, close the tab.

Why “Loose Parts Collective”

In 1971 the architect Simon Nicholson wrote about the theory of loose parts: the idea that places full of movable, open-ended bits invite far more creativity than places where everything is fixed down. Each tool on this site is meant to be a tray of loose parts. The collective is anyone who plays with them, suggests one, or builds one.

Who’s behind it

digitalplay.work was started by Craig Stanley in 2026, building on Manifesto Machines, a set of more than eighty free music instruments. It’s an arts project, not a business. If you’d like to help, the community page explains how.
