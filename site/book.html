Digital Play, the book

The book behind the name.

A short summary, in our own words, of the book that gave this project its name. It isn’t a substitute for reading it.

What it is

Digital Play: Computer games and language aims is a practical book for language teachers, written by Kyle Mawer and Graham Stanley and published in 2011 by Delta Publishing as part of the Delta Teacher Development Series. It won the British Council’s 2012 ELTon award for innovation in teacher resources. It sets out to show teachers that the games their learners already love can be the centre of a lesson, not a reward at the end of it.

How it is put together

The book comes in three parts.

  • The ideas. The first part explains why games and language learning fit together, what makes a game worth using, and what the teacher’s job becomes when the computer is involved.
  • The activities. The middle and biggest part is a bank of practical classroom activities, arranged by how much technology you have, from none at all, through one computer and a projector, to a room full of machines or a class with phones.
  • Taking it further. The last part is about the teacher’s own development: fitting games into a course, finding and judging new ones, and sharing what works with colleagues.

The main ideas, as we understand them

  • Play is serious. People of all ages learn by playing, and a game that holds someone’s attention is doing a lot of the teacher’s work already.
  • Use real games. Ordinary, popular games, played for their own sake, often make better lessons than software made specially to teach. The learning comes from the tasks the teacher builds around them.
  • Talk around the screen. Games naturally create gaps in information, with one person knowing what another needs. That gives learners a real reason to speak, ask, explain and write.
  • Meaning before form. Get people communicating first, then look at the language they needed and used.
  • Before, during and after. Much of the value is in what happens around the game: predicting, planning and instructing beforehand, then describing, retelling and reflecting afterwards.
  • The teacher as guide. Learners may know the games better than the teacher. That’s fine; the teacher brings the language and the structure, and the learners bring the expertise in the game.
  • Any kit will do. You don’t need a computer suite. One screen and a good idea can be enough. What you do with the technology matters much more than what you have.
  • Share what works. Teachers get further when they collect, tag and swap game-based ideas and materials with each other.

From language aims to creative arts

The book is about language teaching. digitalplay.work borrows its spirit and points it at making things: music, art, design, code, writing and the rest. The same habits carry over. Start with something genuinely playful. Bring your own material to it. Talk about what you made and how. Share it so someone else can build on it. Each tool’s LEARN panel is our version of the conversation around the screen.
