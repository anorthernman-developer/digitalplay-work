Learn · Mirror Garden

What it does

Draw one line and watch it bloom into a pattern. Every stroke is copied round a centre point, from two-fold to twenty-four-fold, with or without mirrors. Paint with plain colours or with the colours of your own photo.

Two kinds of symmetry

Rotational symmetry copies a shape round a point, like the spokes of a wheel. Reflective symmetry flips it across a line, like a butterfly. Turn Mirror on and you get both, which is how a kaleidoscope works: two or three mirrors set at an angle multiply whatever sits between them.

Old ideas, everywhere

The Scottish scientist David Brewster patented the kaleidoscope in 1817 and it became a craze within months. Long before that, people in South Asia were drawing rangoli and kolam patterns on doorsteps with rice flour and chalk, often rebuilt every morning. Islamic geometric art builds huge patterns from a compass, a ruler and a few repeated rules.

Why it feels good

Symmetry turns a scribble into something that looks deliberate, so it takes the fear out of drawing. Once you are relaxed, try breaking it: switch to an odd number of folds, or turn the mirrors off halfway.

Try this

  1. Set 6 folds with Mirror on and draw only tiny dots near the centre.
  2. Load a photo, choose Photo brush and draw slowly across the whole canvas.
  3. Start the wandering brush, then change the folds while it draws.
  4. Make a pattern you could print on a tile, then export it.
